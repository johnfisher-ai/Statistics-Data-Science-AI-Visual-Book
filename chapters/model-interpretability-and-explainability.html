Model Interpretability & Explainability

A model that predicts well but cannot say why is a liability, unauditable, undebuggable, and in regulated uses such as lending, hard to defend. This chapter opens the black box with permutation importance and partial dependence for the global picture, and SHAP and LIME to justify a single decision, so you can answer "why?" as well as "what does the model predict?"

📊 Chapter 117

The most accurate models, gradient-boosted forests and deep networks, are the least transparent, because hundreds of trees or millions of weights offer no readable rule. Yet a bank that denies a loan, a hospital that flags a patient, or a court that scores a defendant must be able to explain the decision. Interpretability is how we get accuracy and accountability.

?
Interpretability methods explain a model's behavior. Global methods (permutation importance, partial dependence) describe the model overall; local methods (SHAP, LIME) explain a single prediction as a set of per-feature contributions.
🔍
The chapter in one line

Explain a model globally (which features matter, and how, via permutation importance and partial dependence) and locally (why this one prediction, via SHAP and LIME), because trust, debugging, fairness, and the law all demand a reason to go with the number.

1

Why interpretability matters and the two scales of explanation

An unexplained model cannot be trusted, debugged, or defended. Interpretability serves four concrete needs: trust (would you accept a diagnosis with no reasoning?), debugging (is the model right for the wrong reason, leaning on a leaked or biased feature?), fairness (does it discriminate?), and regulation (laws increasingly require reasons for automated decisions).

Explanations come at two scales, and you need both.

Global explanations describe how the model behaves across all data, its overall priorities and learned relationships. Local explanations justify one specific prediction, and this chapter builds both. A loan applicant does not care which features matter on average; they want to know why they were denied.

2

Global explanations with permutation importance and partial dependence

Permutation importance answers which features matter. It shuffles one feature's values, breaking its link to the target, and measures how far the model's accuracy falls. A big drop means the model relies on it heavily. It is model-agnostic and more trustworthy than a tree's built-in impurity importances. On the credit model, credit score leads by a wide margin (shuffling it costs about 0.07 of accuracy), with income and debt-to-income next at about 0.02 each, the ranking a credit analyst would expect.

Partial dependence answers how a feature acts. It sweeps one feature across its range and plots the model's average output, which for this gradient-boosting model is the log-odds of default. In the notebook, predicted default falls as credit score rises and climbs with debt-to-income, non-linear curves whose steepest regions reveal the model's decision thresholds. (Its per-instance cousin, the ICE plot, draws one line per applicant to check whether the effect is the same for everyone.) Together they give the model's overall logic, but they cannot explain a single denial, which needs a local method.

3

Local explanations with SHAP and LIME

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) borrows a fair-credit-assignment idea from game theory and splits each prediction into an additive contribution from every feature, in a mathematically consistent way. A single applicant's prediction becomes a running total that starts at the average and adds each feature's push, up toward default or down toward repayment, until it reaches their score.

The waterfall above is what you hand a customer or an auditor, because it gives a ranked, quantitative reason for the denial. A large loan (+1.56 log-odds) and a low credit score (+1.40) did most of the damage, debt-to-income added a little more (+0.39), and having no late payments was the only push the other way (−0.20). SHAP's pushes add up exactly on the log-odds scale the model works on, so the waterfall ends at 2.32, which is the 0.91 probability. LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) reaches the same goal differently. It probes the black box with small perturbations around one applicant and fits a simple linear model to that neighborhood. Here LIME's top three features are the same as SHAP's (credit score, debt-to-income and loan size), though in a different order. That agreement is a useful consistency check. It does not prove either explanation faithful to the model, and LIME's weights shift with its random seed and the width of the neighborhood it samples.

That is how a black-box model becomes something you can trust, debug, and defend in front of a regulator.

🤖
Why this matters for AI research

The more powerful the model, the more it needs explaining, and the harder it gets. Rules such as the GDPR, which gives people rights over decisions made solely by automated means, including meaningful information about the logic involved, and US fair-lending rules, which require a lender to state the main reasons it denied credit, push automated decisions toward being explainable. SHAP-style reason codes are now common in banking and insurance. (This describes the general principle and is not legal advice.) At the frontier, an entire field of mechanistic interpretability tries to reverse-engineer what individual neurons and attention heads inside large language models actually compute, work that is central to AI safety, because we cannot align or trust a system we do not understand. The gap between a model's capability and our ability to explain it is one of the defining problems of modern AI, and the tools in this chapter are where learning to close it begins.
