Association rule mining finds what goes with what¶

'Customers who bought this also bought...' Behind that everyday phrase is association rule mining, which scans millions of baskets to find items that co-occur far more than chance. This notebook works library-first with mlxtend. It mines grocery transactions with Apriori and FP-Growth, defines support, confidence, and lift, and shows why lift can separate a real pattern from a popular-item mirage when confidence cannot.

DEMO 2 · SUPPORT
How often does an item (or set) appear?
Support is just the fraction of baskets containing it.
In [4]:

bread appears in 33% of baskets; diapers in 15%

Support = popularity. The support of an itemset is the fraction of all baskets that contain it. Bread shows up in a third of trips; nicher items far less. Support matters for two reasons. Rare itemsets are statistically flimsy (too few baskets to trust), and, as we will see, support is what makes mining every possible combination computationally feasible. We only chase patterns that clear a minimum support.

DEMO 3 · APRIORI: FREQUENT ITEMSETS
Find every set that clears min support
Apriori prunes early and prunes hard.

Why Apriori is fast. There are astronomically many possible itemsets, so checking them all is hopeless. Apriori relies on the downward-closure principle, which says that if an itemset is infrequent, every larger set containing it must also be infrequent, so it can be pruned without checking. Starting from single items and growing only the survivors, Apriori explores a tiny fraction of the space. Here it finds the frequent pairs and triples above 3% support in a blink.

DEMO 4 · RULES: CONFIDENCE AND LIFT
Turn itemsets into 'if A then B'
Three numbers decide whether a rule is worth acting on.

The three metrics. A rule A -> B is scored by support (how often A and B occur together, is it common enough to matter?), confidence (of baskets with A, what fraction also have B, how reliable is the rule?), and lift (how much more likely B is given A, versus B's baseline rate). Lift > 1 means A and B turn up together more often than independence would predict in these baskets, lift = 1 means independence, and lift < 1 means the items repel. A lift is an estimate from the sample, so on modest counts a lift a little above 1 can be chance. The top rules here, like {chips, soda} -> salsa, have lift above 5.

The popular-consequent trap
Confidence ignores how common B already is; lift does not.

Confidence alone can mislead. In bananas -> bread, 37% of banana baskets also contain bread, which sounds like something until you notice bread is in 33% of every basket anyway. Bananas barely change the odds, so its lift is about 1.1, and a Fisher exact test on the counts cannot tell it from independence, a coincidence driven by bread's popularity. Now contrast diapers -> beer, where a lift around 5 means baskets with diapers hold beer about five times as often as the average basket. That pairing was planted in this teaching dataset to echo the retail legend, so it shows the method at work and says nothing about real shoppers. Lift is confidence measured against the baseline, which is why you rank rules by lift, then filter by support to keep them common enough to matter.

Reading the rule set, and scaling it. Each dot is one rule. The brightest dots, the highest-lift rules, sit at middling support (about 0.08 to 0.12), while the most common pairs at the far right involve bread and have a lift near 2, because bread is in so many baskets anyway. This is how an analyst scans hundreds of rules at once and picks the few worth a promotion or a shelf change. FP-Growth is an alternative algorithm that finds the same frequent itemsets as Apriori (the check above confirms it here) but builds a compact tree instead of scanning repeatedly, so it is much faster on large catalogs, the go-to for real retail data.

Association rule mining in one view¶

  • Support = fraction of baskets containing an itemset (popularity, and the key to fast pruning).
  • Confidence = P(B given A); reliable-looking but inflated when B is popular.
  • Lift = how many times more likely B is given A vs its baseline; > 1 means they co-occur more than chance would predict, worth checking against the counts when the lift is small.
  • Apriori prunes with the downward-closure principle; FP-Growth gets the same rules faster.
  • Rank by lift, filter by support, then act by cross-selling, bundling, or re-shelving.
