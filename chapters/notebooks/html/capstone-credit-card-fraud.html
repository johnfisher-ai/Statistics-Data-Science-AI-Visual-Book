Step 1 · The decision, and what each error costs¶

In [2]:
for line in plan.analysis_plan.fillna(""):
    print(line)

AGREED WITH FRAUD OPERATIONS BEFORE ANY MODEL WAS FITTED.

THE DECISION. A flagged transaction is held and reviewed by an analyst. An unflagged one settles.
The model produces a queue, and the team can work about 400 reviews a day.

THE COST OF BEING WRONG, from operations, in dollars:
  a missed fraud        = the transaction amount, written off, plus 25 of handling
  a false positive      = 4 of analyst time, plus an estimated 12 of customer annoyance
  These are NOT symmetric and they are not even a fixed ratio: the cost of a miss depends
  on the amount. That rules out any single global threshold chosen from a curve.

M1 SPLIT: by time. The model will score future transactions, so it is validated on later ones.
   A random split would let it learn from transactions that had not happened yet.
M2 METRIC: PRECISION-RECALL AUC as the headline, never ROC-AUC. With 0.27 percent positives
   the false positive rate has a denominator of 120,000 and barely moves, so ROC flatters.
M3 IMBALANCE: three approaches compared on the SAME model and split, and judged on expected
   cost rather than on any ranking metric: (a) random oversampling of the minority, (b) class
   weights, (c) nothing at all, and tune the threshold.
M4 CALIBRATION: checked for each approach. Resampling and class weights both change the base
   rate the model believes in, so a probability from them is not a probability.
M5 THRESHOLD: from the cost matrix and the review capacity, not from Youden or an F1 peak.
M6 EXPLANATION: SHAP on the flagged transactions, used first as a LEAKAGE CHECK and only
   afterwards as an explanation for the analyst working the queue.
M7 EXCLUSIONS: duplicate rows, distance sentinels of -1, and zero-amount authorization holds.

WHAT WE ARE NOT DOING: reporting accuracy. Flagging nothing is 99.73 percent accurate.

The cost of a miss depends on the transaction. That single sentence rules out choosing a threshold from any curve, because every such rule assumes both errors have a fixed price. Here a missed 8-dollar coffee and a missed 2,000-dollar laptop cost very different amounts, and the threshold has to know that.

What this shows. Fraud is one transaction in four hundred on the training window. That single number governs every choice in the rest of the notebook: which metric means anything, where the threshold goes, and what a reviewer's hour is worth.

The comparison table is the honest version of what a model has to work with. A new device is the strongest single signal at 1.7 standard deviations, with distance from home, transaction size, being online and being foreign all some way behind it. Step 5 · The leak, found before anything else¶

The warehouse table carries a column called chargeback_code_filed. It looks like a feature. Fit with it and see.

def fit_lr(cols, train, y, cols_mu=None, cols_sd=None):
    m_, s_ = (train[cols].mean(), train[cols].std()) if cols_mu is None else (cols_mu, cols_sd)
    mod = LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000).fit((train[cols]-m_)/s_, y)
    return mod, m_, s_

The linear model wins at the settings tried. Each ensemble was fitted once at a reasonable hand-set configuration, with no search, so tuning might close some of the gap. Two genuine interactions sit in the data-generating process, so there is real non-additive structure for a tree to find. It cannot find it from 223 fraudulent transactions. A human who suspects that a new device on a card-not-present sale is worse than either alone can add that in one line, and does better than either ensemble.

This is the opener's baseline rule doing real work. Flexible models need positives, not rows, and 90,000 rows containing 223 positives is a small dataset wearing a large one's clothes.

Resampling, class weights, and leaving it alone and moving the threshold. Same model, same split, same features.

The three PR-AUCs agree within 0.0004. They rank the transactions almost the same way, because all three fit the same model to the same information; what resampling and class weights change is not the ordering but the base rate the model believes in.

And that is fatal here. Both put the average predicted fraud rate at about 11.5 percent when the truth is 0.32 percent, a factor of thirty-six. A number like that cannot be multiplied by a transaction amount to get an expected loss, which is the only calculation the operations team asked for. The remedy that does nothing is the one that leaves a probability behind.

Step 8 · The threshold, from the cost matrix¶

In [10]:
C_FP = 4 + 12          # analyst time plus customer annoyance
amt = te.amount.values

def total_cost(p, t, y=None, a=None):
    y = yte if y is None else y
    a = amt if a is None else a
    flag = p >= t
    fp = int(((flag) & (y == 0)).sum()) * C_FP
    fn = float((a[(~flag) & (y == 1)] + 25).sum())
    return fp + fn, int(flag.sum()), int(((flag) & (y == 1)).sum())

# choose the threshold on the training window, from out-of-fold predictions,
# so the test window is only used to score it
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_predict, StratifiedKFold
oof = cross_val_predict(LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000), Xtr, ytr, method="predict_proba",
                        cv=StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0))[:, 1]
amt_tr = tr.amount.values
bt = min((total_cost(oof, t, ytr, amt_tr)[0], t) for t in np.unique(np.round(oof, 5)))[1]
print(f"false positive = ${C_FP}    missed fraud = the amount + $25")
print(f"threshold chosen on out-of-fold training predictions: {bt:.4f}\n")

print(f"{'threshold':>10}{'flagged':>9}{'caught':>8}{'missed':>8}{'cost':>12}   on the test window")
for t in (0.002, 0.01, 0.05, bt, 0.15, 0.20, 0.40):
    c, fl, ca = total_cost(p_plain, t)
    print(f"{t:>10.4f}{fl:>9}{ca:>8}{yte.sum()-ca:>8}{c:>12,.0f}")

bc, _, _ = total_cost(p_plain, bt)
c_none, _, _ = total_cost(p_plain, 2.0)
print(f"\nflag nothing                          ${c_none:,.0f}")

fl = int((p_plain >= bt).sum())
print(f"which flags {fl} transactions over 30 days, about {fl/30:.0f} a day")

bc_h, bt_h = min((total_cost(p_plain, t)[0], t) for t in np.unique(np.round(p_plain, 5)))
print(f"\nthe best threshold in hindsight on the test window is {bt_h:.4f}, at ${bc_h:,.0f};")
print("no live system could have chosen it in advance, so it is a ceiling, not a result")

flag_a = p_plain*(amt + 25) > (1 - p_plain)*C_FP

The threshold comes from the training window. Chosen on out-of-fold training predictions it is 0.101, and on the test window it costs 10,381 dollars, 28 percent less than flagging nothing, at about four flags a day. The best threshold in hindsight, 0.0883, would have cost 9,959, but picking it needs the test answers, so it is a ceiling rather than a result.

The plan said a single threshold was the wrong shape for this cost matrix, and the last lines bear it out. Flagging a transaction when its expected loss, p times the amount plus 25, exceeds the expected cost of a review, (1 - p) times 16, needs no tuning at all and costs 9,181 dollars, less than any single threshold. That is the rule to deploy, and it works only because the untouched model's probabilities are roughly calibrated.

Filling the queue costs 13 times more than having no model.

The review capacity is not a target to fill. Working every case the team physically could catches 93 of the 95 frauds and costs thirteen times more than ignoring fraud entirely, because 11,900 of those 12,000 reviews are of legitimate transactions at $16 each. The amount-aware rule uses about one percent of the available capacity.

That is worth carrying to the operations meeting, because "we have capacity for 400 a day" is usually stated as though it were the binding constraint. Here the binding constraint is the price of being wrong.

Step 11 · Ethics, bias, and limits¶

  • Never report accuracy on a 0.3 percent outcome. Flagging nothing scores 99.68 percent, and any model that beats it by a decimal has told you nothing.
  • ROC-AUC flatters when negatives dominate. With 29,000 legitimate transactions in the denominator the false positive rate barely moves, so use precision-recall and quote the base rate beside it.
  • Rebalancing buys ranking and sells calibration. If the downstream step multiplies the probability by anything, resampling and class weights have broken it, and no ranking metric will say so.
  • A flagged transaction is a person having their card declined. The $12 in the cost matrix stands for a real customer stranded at a checkout, and it is a placeholder for a harm that is not evenly distributed. Customers who travel, who shop late, or who have just replaced a phone will be flagged more often, and none of those are wrongdoing.
  • Check the flag rate by group before deploying. This analysis reports one threshold for everybody. A rule that flags one segment at several times the rate of another needs to be examined rather than shipped, and Capstone 41, the fairness audit, is about how.
  • Leakage is a data-warehouse problem, not a modeling one. The fix is a documented feature store that records when each field becomes available, not a smarter analyst noticing in time.
  • The model ages. Fraud is adversarial: the patterns here were learned from days 1 to 90 and the people producing them adapt. Anything shipped needs its performance re-measured on a schedule, not when somebody wonders.

From analysis to report. The notebook carries the chain: the costs before the model, the accuracy trap, a time split, a leak caught before anything else, a bake-off the linear baseline wins, three imbalance remedies that rank identically and disagree by a factor of 36 on the level, a threshold chosen on the training window and the amount-aware rule the cost matrix implies, and the review capacity shown not to be the constraint. The written reports turn it into a policy the fraud team can run and a technical account a fellow statistician can audit. Automate the evidence; author the argument.
