E-commerce Conversions: the Bernoulli Trial 🛒

Every web session is a single yes/no experiment: add to cart, or not. This is the Bernoulli distribution in the wild. We find the baseline conversion rate p, model it, break it down by device, and simulate when the next cart addition will arrive.

BEAT 1 · THE BASELINE RATE
One number that runs the business
A Bernoulli trial has two outcomes with probabilities p and 1-p. Here the trial is a session and the success is cart_addition = 1. The single parameter p is the conversion rate.
In [2]:
x = df["cart_addition"]
p = x.mean()
print(f"sessions          : {len(x):,}")
print(f"add-to-cart events : {int(x.sum()):,}")
print(f"baseline rate p    : {p:.4f}  ({p:.1%})")
print(f"Bernoulli mean  = p        = {p:.4f}")
print(f"Bernoulli variance = p(1-p) = {p*(1-p):.4f}")
print(f"std dev = sqrt(p(1-p))      = {np.sqrt(p*(1-p)):.4f}")

The whole distribution is two bars: about 85% of sessions add nothing, 15% convert. That single rate p is the heartbeat of the funnel, and its variance p(1−p) is largest when p is near 0.5 and small when conversions are rare, as here.

BEAT 2 · BREAK IT DOWN BY DEVICE
Is the trial the same everywhere?
A Bernoulli rate can hide segments. Splitting p by device shows whether mobile, desktop, and tablet visitors convert at different rates, the first question any analyst asks.
In [4]:
by_dev = df.groupby("device_type")["cart_addition"].agg(["mean","count"]).sort_values("mean",ascending=False)
by_dev.columns=["conversion_rate","sessions"]
print(by_dev.round(4))
chi2, pval, dof, _ = stats.chi2_contingency(pd.crosstab(df["device_type"], df["cart_addition"]))
print(f"\nchi-square test, device vs conversion: chi2 = {chi2:.2f}, df = {dof}, p = {pval:.3f}")

Each device is its own Bernoulli process with its own p. A segment that sits clearly above or below the dashed overall rate is a lead worth testing. Here the chi-square test of device against conversion gives p = 0.052, just short of the usual 5% line, so the tablet gap (on only 84 sessions) needs more traffic before the business chases it. BEAT 3 · WHEN WILL THE NEXT CART ADDITION ARRIVE?
Bernoulli trials become a waiting game
Stream sessions one after another, each a Bernoulli(p) trial. The number of sessions until the next add-to-cart follows the GEOMETRIC distribution, with mean 1/p. We simulate it.
In [6]:
rng = np.random.default_rng(48)
# simulate a long stream of sessions; record the gap (in sessions) between conversions
stream = rng.random(2_000_000) < p
idx = np.flatnonzero(stream)
gaps = np.diff(idx)                      # sessions between consecutive conversions
print(f"simulated mean gap = {gaps.mean():.2f} sessions   (theory 1/p = {1/p:.2f})")
print(f"P(next conversion within 5 sessions) = {1-(1-p)**5:.3f}")
print(f"P(next conversion within 10 sessions) = {1-(1-p)**10:.3f}")

simulated mean gap = 6.76 sessions   (theory 1/p = 6.76)
P(next conversion within 5 sessions) = 0.551
P(next conversion within 10 sessions) = 0.798
In [7]:
fig,ax=plt.subplots(figsize=(7,3.2))
kmax=40; ax.hist(gaps,bins=np.arange(1,kmax+1),density=True,color=AMBER,alpha=0.7,label="simulated gaps")
k=np.arange(1,kmax); ax.plot(k, stats.geom.pmf(k,p),"o-",color=TEAL,ms=4,label="Geometric(p) PMF")
ax.axvline(1/p,color=PINK,ls="--",lw=2,label=f"mean = 1/p = {1/p:.1f}")
ax.set_xlabel("sessions until next add-to-cart"); ax.set_ylabel("probability"); ax.set_title("When will the next cart addition arrive?"); ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

The simulated waiting times trace the geometric PMF closely, with an average wait of about 7 sessions between conversions. The business can now answer "how long until the next cart addition?" probabilistically: there is a 55% chance within 5 sessions and a 80% chance within 10. Bernoulli trials, stacked in time, became a forecast.

BEAT 4 · SCALING UP TO A DAY
From one trial to a daily total
Add up n independent Bernoulli trials and you get a BINOMIAL count. A day of n sessions yields Binomial(n, p) conversions, whose total is, by the CLT, approximately normal.
In [8]:
n_day = 200
expected = n_day*p
print(f"in a day of {n_day} sessions: expected conversions = n*p = {expected:.1f}")
print(f"std dev = sqrt(n p (1-p)) = {np.sqrt(n_day*p*(1-p)):.2f}")
sim_days = rng.binomial(n_day, p, size=100_000)
print(f"simulated daily mean = {sim_days.mean():.1f}, P(>=40 conversions) = {(sim_days>=40).mean():.3f}")

in a day of 200 sessions: expected conversions = n*p = 29.6
std dev = sqrt(n p (1-p)) = 5.02

simulated daily mean = 29.6, P(>=40 conversions) = 0.028

A single yes/no trial scales all the way up: one session is Bernoulli, a fixed batch of them is binomial, and a long run of daily totals is approximately normal (the Central Limit Theorem, Chapter 58). The same p that described one click now forecasts the whole day, with an expected 30 conversions per 200 sessions.

✅ Case closed
A web session is the textbook Bernoulli trial. We estimated its rate p = 0.148, modeled its mean and variance, segmented it by device, simulated the geometric waiting time to the next cart addition, and scaled it to a binomial daily total. One parameter, the whole conversion story.
