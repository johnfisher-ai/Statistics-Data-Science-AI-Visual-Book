def load_clean():
    try: df = pd.read_csv('../../data/bike_share.csv')
    except FileNotFoundError: df = pd.read_csv(BASE + 'bike_share.csv')
    df = df.dropna(subset=['rentals']).drop_duplicates(subset='date'); df['rentals']=df['rentals'].astype(int)
    norm = lambda s: s.astype(str).str.strip().str.lower()
    df['season'] = norm(df['season']).str.capitalize()
    df['weather'] = norm(df['weather']).map({'clear':'Clear','mist':'Mist','light rain':'Light Rain','heavy rain':'Heavy Rain'})
    df['workingday'] = df['workingday'].astype(str).str.strip().str.upper().map({'1':1,'YES':1,'0':0,'NO':0})
    for c in ['humidity','windspeed']: df[c]=pd.to_numeric(df[c],errors='coerce'); df[c]=df[c].fillna(df[c].median())
    dt = pd.to_datetime(df['date'], format='mixed', errors='coerce'); df['is_weekend']=(dt.dt.dayofweek>=5).astype(int)
    return df
df = load_clean()
FORM = 'rentals ~ temp_c + I((temp_c-14)**2) + humidity + windspeed + C(weather) + C(season) + workingday + holiday'
pois = smf.glm(FORM, df, family=sm.families.Poisson()).fit()
nb = smf.glm(FORM, df, family=sm.families.NegativeBinomial(alpha=1/6)).fit()
print(f'n={len(df)}  NB pseudo-R2={1-nb.deviance/nb.null_deviance:.3f}  NB AIC={nb.aic:,.0f}  Poisson AIC={pois.aic:,.0f}')

What we learn. The rate ratios are essentially the same under both models, but the negative-binomial standard errors are several times larger than Poisson's across the board. Under overdispersion, Poisson's error bars are fiction that would make weak effects look significant and tight forecasts look certain. The negative binomial reports the honest uncertainty. This is exactly the count-model parallel of using robust standard errors for heteroscedasticity.

TAKE IT FURTHER 2 · DOES TEMPERATURE REALLY CURVE?
Is the squared term worth it?
Compare AIC with and without it, and plot the fitted temperature response.

nb_lin = smf.glm('rentals ~ temp_c + humidity + windspeed + C(weather) + C(season) + workingday + holiday', df, family=sm.families.NegativeBinomial(alpha=1/6)).fit()

AIC  with temp^2 = 11,083   without = 11,098   (lower is better)

What we learn. The squared term earns its place, since the AIC is lower with it and the curve shows demand rising with warmth, more slowly in the heat (its peak would sit above the warmest day in the data), which the straight-line version misses. This is the flexible-model idea from earlier chapters applied inside a GLM, where a quadratic feature lets a linear model bend.

Why I((temp_c-14)**2) subtracts 14. We center the temperature at the middle of the data (the average day is about 14°C) before squaring. Centering removes most of the correlation between the linear temp_c term and its square (in this data it falls from about +0.97 to roughly 0), keeping both coefficients stable and readable. It does not change the fitted curve or the accuracy (the linear term absorbs the shift); any value near the mean works. The Chapter 105 main notebook has a plot verifying that 14 is the center. I(...) just tells the formula parser to square the number literally rather than read ** as a formula operator.

TAKE IT FURTHER 3 · TEST ON A LATER YEAR
Is it overfit?
Train on 2011 and test on 2012, because a forecast is judged on later data.
In [6]:
X = pd.get_dummies(df[['temp_c','humidity','windspeed','weather','season','workingday','holiday','is_weekend']], drop_first=True).astype(float)
X['temp_sq'] = (df['temp_c']-14)**2
# forecasting needs a time-ordered split: train on 2011, test on 2012, and score both with the same R2
year = pd.to_datetime(df['date'], format='mixed', errors='coerce').dt.year
tr, te = df[year==2011], df[year==2012]
fit = smf.glm(FORM, tr, family=sm.families.NegativeBinomial(alpha=1/6)).fit()
r2 = lambda d: 1 - ((d.rentals - fit.predict(d))**2).sum() / ((d.rentals - d.rentals.mean())**2).sum()
print(f'trained on 2011 ({len(tr)} days): R2 = {r2(tr):.3f}   tested on 2012 ({len(te)} days): R2 = {r2(te):.3f}')

trained on 2011 (363 days): R2 = 0.548   tested on 2012 (363 days): R2 = 0.557

What we learn. Trained on 2011 and scored on 2012 with the same R-squared, the model does about as well on the new year (0.557) as on the year it learned from (0.548), so it generalizes forward in time. A forecast must be tested on later data than it trained on, because a random split lets the model peek at the future, and a big gap would have warned of overfitting. Always check out-of-sample before trusting a forecast operationally.

What we learn. We tested the idea rather than assuming it, and the simpler main-effects model stands.

Turn the model into a planning tool
Feed a 7-day weather forecast and predict each day's demand with a range.

forecast = pd.DataFrame({
  'day':['Mon','Tue','Wed','Thu','Fri','Sat','Sun'],
  'temp_c':[24,25,19,17,22,26,23], 'humidity':[55,52,70,80,58,50,60], 'windspeed':[8,10,15,20,9,7,11],
  'weather':['Clear','Clear','Light Rain','Heavy Rain','Mist','Clear','Clear'],
  'season':['Summer']*7, 'workingday':[1,1,1,1,1,0,0], 'holiday':[0]*7})
pr = nb.get_prediction(forecast).summary_frame(alpha=0.05)
forecast['predicted'] = pr['mean'].round(0).astype(int)
# a 95% range for one actual day: the negative-binomial spread around each mean (weather inputs taken as known)
from scipy.stats import nbinom
size = 6.0   # 1/alpha
forecast['low'] = nbinom.ppf(0.025, size, size/(size+pr['mean'])).astype(int)
forecast['high'] = nbinom.ppf(0.975, size, size/(size+pr['mean'])).astype(int)
print(forecast[['day','weather','temp_c','predicted','low','high']].to_string(index=False))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8,4))

What we learn. As a planning tool, the model forecasts far more rentals for a warm, clear day than for the cold heavy-rain day, and the mid-week storm is clearly the low point to schedule maintenance. Each bar comes with a 95% range for the actual count on that day, which assumes the weather forecast itself is right, so operations can plan for the likely spread instead of a false-precision single number.

The demand model in plain terms¶

  • Weather is the biggest lever on demand. A rainy day can cut rentals by up to three-quarters, and warmer days are busier (about +33% per 5°C in mild weather, less when it is already warm).
  • The calendar matters too, since working days are busier than weekends and holidays are quieter than either.
  • Daily counts are volatile, far more than a naive model assumes, so we used a negative-binomial model that reports honest confidence, and a test on 2012, after training on 2011, confirmed it forecasts a new year about as well.
  • The extensions reinforced the story. The temperature curve is real, the negative binomial's error bars are the trustworthy ones, and the data give no clear evidence that weather hits weekends and weekdays differently.

The Step 12 plain-English write-up¶

What we did. We took two years of daily rental records, cleaned them, and built a formula that forecasts how many bikes will be rented on a given day from the weather and the calendar, then stress-tested it five ways.

How good is it? It explains a little over half of the day-to-day variance in rentals (R-squared about 0.55) and holds up on a year it has not seen, so it can plan a future week as well as describe the past.

What drives demand: weather first (rain and cold cut it sharply), then temperature, then the calendar.

How sure are we? We used a model built for volatile counts (so the confidence is honest), confirmed the temperature effect curves, checked it generalizes, and produced a week-ahead forecast with ranges, ready to drive tomorrow's bike-rebalancing and staffing plan.

Let the weather forecast set the bike plan, scaling down for rain and cold and up for warm clear working days, and using the low-demand rainy days for maintenance.
