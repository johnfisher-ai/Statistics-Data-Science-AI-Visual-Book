The Regression Workflow

Four decisions fix what a coefficient means, and every one of them is made before anybody reads it. The outcome's own distribution, what belongs on the right-hand side, the order you check assumptions in, and the decision the number has to serve.

⏱️ ~16 min read
🎯 The workflow
📊 Chapter 185

Part XV taught how to fit a regression and read what came out. This part is about the four decisions that fix what the output means before anybody reads it, each of which can be made wrongly by a model that fits beautifully and reports nothing amiss.

β
A regression coefficient is not a fact about the world. It is the answer to a very specific question that four earlier decisions have already asked on your behalf. Change any one of them and the same data returns a different number, with no warning in the output that the question has changed.
⭐
What this part is for

Four projects, each built around one of the four decisions. In every one of them the model fits, the software reports no error, and the naive answer is wrong: by a factor of six, by the deletion of the finding, by a factor of two in predicted risk, and by a reversed sign.

1

What This Part Adds

Part XV covered the mechanics: fit the model, check the residuals, read the coefficient with its interval. Those five steps still apply and are not repeated here. What Part XV largely assumed is the part that goes wrong most often in practice.

Part XV askedThis part asks
How do I fit this model?Is this the right family for the outcome I have?
What do the coefficients mean?Which variables am I entitled to put beside them?
Do the assumptions hold?In what order must I check, given that each failure hides the next?
Is the effect significant?What decision does this number feed, and what does that decision actually need?

Every question in the right-hand column is settled before the model is fitted or immediately after, and none of them is answered by a fit statistic. That is the uncomfortable common thread: R-squared, p-values and AUC are all silent on every one of these decisions, and a model can score well on all three while answering a question nobody asked.

2

Decision One: What Kind of Outcome Is This?

Ordinary least squares assumes a continuous outcome with roughly constant variance. Plenty of outcomes are not that, and forcing them into it produces a model that runs without complaint.

OutcomeFamilyWhat the coefficient meansThe trap
ContinuousGaussianChange in the outcome per unitSkew and non-constant variance
Continuous, multiplicativeGaussian on the logApproximate percentage changeZeros, and the retransformation bias
BinaryBinomial, logit linkLog odds ratioReading an odds ratio as a risk ratio
CountPoisson, log linkLog rate ratioOverdispersion, and the missing offset
Count with wide exposurePoisson with an offsetLog rate ratioWithout the offset you have modeled size
Bounded proportionBeta, or binomial with weightsDepends on the linkPredictions outside [0, 1]
The offset is the one people miss

If the units you are comparing differ in size, a count model without an offset is a model of size. Districts, stores, machines and policy-years all vary by orders of magnitude, and log(exposure) entered with its coefficient fixed at 1 is what converts a statement about counts into a statement about rates. Capstone 28 shows a coefficient that changes sign on this alone.

3

Decision Two: What Belongs on the Right-Hand Side?

This is the decision with the least statistical content and the largest consequences. Three kinds of variable look identical in a regression table and do completely different things.

Three variables, three verdicts, one regression table. Nothing in the output distinguishes them. The arrows come from knowing how the data were produced, and that knowledge is not recoverable from the data.

The rule is short and it is about time, not statistics. A variable measured before the exposure can be a confounder and is usually safe. A variable measured after it is a mediator or a collider and is usually not. Capstone 25 is built on a control that takes a pay gap from 8.1 percent to 2.3 and deletes the mechanism producing most of it, and the regression output looks identical either way.

4

Decision Three: In What Order Do You Check?

Regression diagnostics are taught as a checklist and behave as a sequence. Every diagnostic is computed from the residuals, and the residuals are whatever the current model has failed to explain, so each test measures the assumption it names only as far as the other assumptions hold.

CheckOrderWhy it must come there
Functional formFirstA misspecified mean leaves residuals dominated by systematic error, which makes every later test meaningless
InfluenceSecondA handful of enormous residuals inflates the overall spread enough to hide a variance pattern in all the rest
Constant varianceThirdOnly meaningful once the mean is right and no small group dominates the residuals
Independent errorsLastA smooth misfit produces runs of same-signed residuals that are indistinguishable from correlated errors

Run the four in parallel on a first-draft model and you will find some subset of the faults that are present, and you will not know which you missed. Capstone 26 runs them that way deliberately: three violations are present, and the parallel pass reports one.

5

Decision Four: What Is the Number For?

The same fitted model supports three quite different products, and each one is judged by something the others do not need.

The productWhat mattersWhat does not settle it
An effect estimateThe coefficient, its interval, and what was adjusted forPredictive accuracy
A predictionOut-of-sample error, and an honest prediction intervalThe significance of any coefficient
A decision ruleCalibration, the threshold, and the cost ratio behind itDiscrimination, which cannot see the level at all

The third row is the one that catches people. A model that ranks cases correctly can be wrong about the level of risk by a factor of two, and AUC is mathematically incapable of detecting it, because it reads only the order. Capstone 27 puts two models with the same AUC to four decimal places against a real clinical threshold and gets 951 extra admissions out of one of them.

A threshold is a cost ratio in disguise

Youden's index, the elbow of the ROC curve and "0.5" all encode a statement about how much worse one error is than the other. None of them asked anybody what that ratio should be. If a missed case is forty times worse than a false alarm, the threshold is 1/(1+40), and that arithmetic belongs to whoever owns the consequence rather than to the analyst.

6

What the Fit Statistics Cannot Tell You

Worth stating plainly, because it is the reason all four of these decisions have to be made deliberately rather than discovered.

The decisionGoes wrong silently asDetected by
Wrong outcome familyA model that runs, fits and reports significanceA dispersion test, or predictions outside the possible range
A mediator on the right-hand sideA smaller coefficient and a much better R²Asking whether the control is assigned after the exposure
Diagnostics in the wrong orderThree of four tests passingRe-running them after every repair
Wrong productAn excellent AUC on a rule that misprices the decisionCalibration, and net benefit against doing nothing

In each row the middle column is the danger. None of these failures announces itself, and two of them make the output look better: adjusting for a mediator raises R-squared, and a miscalibrated score can look more cautious than a correct one.

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • ✓A coefficient answers a question four earlier decisions already asked. Change one and the same data gives a different number, with no warning in the output.
  • ✓Match the family to the outcome, and remember the offset. A count model without exposure is a model of size.
  • ✓Time settles the right-hand side. Measured before the exposure it can be a confounder; measured after it, it is a mediator or a collider.
  • ✓Diagnostics are a sequence. Fix the mean, then influence, then variance, then dependence, re-running after each repair.
  • ✓Name the product before choosing the metric. An effect estimate, a prediction and a decision rule are judged by three different things.
